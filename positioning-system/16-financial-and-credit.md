## §3. Credit — the mechanics that actually move your score

**[R]** Utilisation sits in FICO's *Amounts Owed* category, roughly **30% of the
score** — second only to payment history. myFICO's guidance: keep it **below 10%**
to build and hold a strong score; below 30% as the general rule; and **not zero**,
because 0% across all cards shows no use of available credit rather than
responsible use. FICO considers **both aggregate utilisation and the highest
utilisation on any single card**.
(<https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/blog/credit-utilization-be>)
Confidence: high — issuer-adjacent primary source.

### The single highest-return action available to you

**[I]** Here is the arithmetic that most people miss. Utilisation is reported at
your **statement closing date**, not your due date. If your total limit is, say,
$4,000 and you run $1,600 of expenses through the cards, your statement can
report **40% utilisation even though you pay in full every month and carry no
debt whatsoever.** Your score is then being penalised for behaviour that is
financially perfect.

**Fix: pay the balance down mid-cycle, before the statement closes.**

1. Find each card's statement closing date (app or statement, not the due date).
2. Two or three days before it, pay the balance down to roughly 1–8% of that
   card's limit.
3. Let the small remainder report, then pay it off by the due date.

**Cost: about ten minutes a month. Effect: potentially tens of points, with no
change to your spending, your budget, or your cash position.** For someone with
modest limits and $1,600 of monthly throughput, this is worth more than every
other credit tactic in this file combined.

**[REC]** Put both statement dates in Google Calendar as recurring events, three
days early, titled "Pay down before statement." Implementation intentions
(`17-evidence-base.md` §6) apply to this as much as to networking.

### Zero is not the target
Letting **one** card report a small balance (1–8% of its limit) while others
report zero is a widely used optimisation. **[I]** Do not over-engineer it — the
difference between this and simply keeping everything under 9% is small and only
matters in the month you apply for something. Get the mid-cycle payment habit
first.

## §4. Your two cards

### Chase Freedom Rise
**[R]** An unsecured starter card; minimum approved limit $500; cardholders are
**automatically considered for a credit-limit increase after six months** of
on-time payments; and are **evaluated every 12 months for automatic upgrade to
Freedom Unlimited**, with **no new credit check** and no change to credit score
or (necessarily) limit.
(<https://www.chase.com/personal/credit-cards/education/basics/freedom-rise-credit-limit-increase>,
<https://www.chase.com/personal/credit-cards/freedom/rise/automatic-upgrade>)

**[I] Why the upgrade path matters more than it looks.** A product change from
Rise to Freedom Unlimited is **not a new account** — it does not add to your
5/24 count and it does not reset the account's age, which continues to build your
average age of accounts. You get a better earning structure while keeping your
oldest US tradeline intact. That is a strictly better outcome than opening a new
card, and it costs you nothing but time.

**[REC]** Do nothing. Pay on time, keep utilisation low, let the automatic
reviews happen. Do not close this card — it is likely your oldest US tradeline
and closing it would eventually damage your average account age.

### Citi Costco Anywhere Visa
No annual fee beyond the Costco membership itself, and a long-term keeper. You
opened it recently, so it is dragging your average account age down temporarily —
this is normal, expected, and reverses with time. **[REC]** Keep it, use it,
leave it alone.

## §5. Should you open a third card? Almost certainly not yet.

### The Chase 5/24 constraint
**[R]** Chase will generally not approve most of its personal cards if you have
opened **five or more personal credit cards from any issuer in the past 24
months**. Never officially published, but consistently observed. Authorised-user
accounts and charge cards count; most business cards do not; **closed accounts
still count** because the rule is based on the open date.

You are at roughly **2/24**. That gives you three slots before Chase closes.

**[I] What those slots are worth.** Chase's Sapphire cards are the standard
reason people manage 5/24 at all, and they are effectively unobtainable once you
cross it. Spending a slot now, on a card you do not need, during a period of zero
income, in exchange for cashback on $19,200 of annual spending, is a poor trade.
Slots are more valuable later, when you have income and a reason.

### The Citi velocity rule
**[R]** Citi's observed "8/65": no more than one Citi card in 8 days and no more
than two in 65 days; ~90 days recommended between business-card applications.
Community-derived, not published by Citi. Relevant only if you were considering a
second Citi product, which you should not be right now.

### Annual-fee arithmetic
At ~$19,200 of annual spending, a $95 annual fee requires about **0.5% of
incremental return across your entire spend** merely to break even — before the
category restrictions that make most premium cards worth less than advertised at
this spending level. **[REC]** No annual-fee cards until you have income and can
show the maths works on your actual statement categories.

### On American Express
**[REC]** Not now. You would be adding a fourth 24-month slot with no income,
and Amex's value concentrates in travel and dining categories that a $1,600/month
budget does not heavily use. Revisit roughly six months after you start working,
when you have income, a longer file, and an actual spending pattern to optimise
against.

### The one condition under which you should open a card sooner
If your **total credit limit is so low that normal spending pushes statement
utilisation above ~30% even with mid-cycle payments**, an additional limit is a
legitimate structural fix. **[REC]** But try in this order first: (1) mid-cycle
payments, (2) request a limit increase on the Citi card, (3) let the Freedom Rise
automatic review run. Only if all three fail should a new account be on the table.

## §6. Credit-limit increase requests

- **Chase Freedom Rise** — automatic review after 6 months [R]. Let it run;
  requesting manually risks a hard inquiry for something you would get anyway.
- **Citi** — CLI requests are commonly available online. **[I]** Whether Citi
  treats it as a soft or hard pull varies; the request page usually discloses
  this before you submit. Read that disclosure, and do not submit if it says hard
  pull unless you have a reason.
- **[REC]** Do not request within about six months of opening an account, and do
  not request more than one increase in any six-month window.

⚠️ CLI requests ask for income. Answer accurately, including whatever your
household situation actually permits you to state. **Do not overstate income on a
credit application** — that is a separate and serious problem from anything else
in this system, and it is one that compounds badly with an open immigration
matter.

## §7. Twelve and twenty-four month plan

**Months 0–6 (now):** protect runway · mid-cycle payments · zero revolving debt ·
no new accounts · let both automatic reviews run · track everything in Sheet 13
weekly.

**Months 6–12 (post-authorisation):** rebuild the emergency fund to 6 months
of the *new* budget · start retirement contributions, capturing any employer
match first (a match is an immediate 50–100% return and there is no comparable
alternative) · only then reassess cards, with income and a real spending pattern.

**Months 12–24:** 12 months of expenses liquid if you are seriously pursuing
acquisition entrepreneurship (see `15-business-preparation.md`) · begin a
dedicated business-capital line, separate from the emergency fund and never
commingled · revisit the 5/24 slots deliberately, for cards you have a reason to
hold.

**[I] The strategic point about all of this.** You said you eventually want to
deploy your own capital. Credit optimisation contributes essentially nothing to
that. What contributes is: a clean file, real savings, and — most of all —
**income from a role in your natural market**. The financial plan is genuinely
subordinate to the career plan here, and the correct amount of time to spend on
points strategy over the next six months is close to zero.
