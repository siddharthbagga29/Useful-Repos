## §1. The two strategic decisions

### Decision 1: Compete on evidence, not on pedigree

Your GPA is 3.24, your degree is from a good regional programme, and your two
valuation-course grades are a C and a C+ [V]. In any process that screens on
credentials — bulge-bracket on-cycle, megafund associate, top-tier consulting —
you lose before a human reads a word.

In processes that screen on *demonstrated work*, you win, because at 23 you have
underwritten acquisitions and written LP materials [V].

**Therefore: never enter a credential-screened process.** Every hour spent on
one is an hour taken from the market where your evidence is decisive. This is
why the ranking in §E puts bulge-bracket at 25 and tells you to spend zero hours
there. It is not modesty; it is allocation.

### Decision 2: Be visibly, permanently, *not a sponsorship problem*

**[I]** In a two-partner sponsor shop, an international candidate is a
non-starter — not on merit, but because the firm has no immigration counsel and
no appetite for a lottery. Your listener will assume you are that candidate.
They will not ask. They will simply not follow up.

**Therefore: pre-empt it, in plain words, early, unprompted.** Once your EAD or
green card is issued: *"I'm a permanent resident track — married to a U.S.
citizen, no sponsorship now or ever."* Before then: *"I'm finishing my green-card
process; I'll be authorised with no sponsorship needed, and I'm using the
interim to build."*

**[I]** This converts your single largest apparent liability into a
differentiator against every other international candidate for the same seat.
Very few candidates think to say it. Do not bury it in a résumé footer where it
reads defensive; say it out loud in conversation where it reads confident.

## §2. Your positioning statement

> **"I am a private-company analyst. I build valuations and underwriting for
> businesses that have no market price and no clean data — and I write the memo
> that makes the decision legible to whoever is putting up the capital."**

Three claims, all [V]:
1. **Valuation without a quote** — DCF and EBITDA comps on illiquid private
   companies, $3.5M base to $21M upside.
2. **Underwriting** — bottoms-up, normalised EBITDA, IRR/MOIC at multiple entry
   and exit points, purchase-price sensitivity.
3. **Writing for capital** — go/no-go memos to a Managing Partner; Reg D
   investor materials for a deal-by-deal PE platform.

Claim 3 is the differentiator. Many people can model. Fewer can write the
document a capital provider actually decides from. Lead with it.

## §3. Story scripts

Rehearse out loud. Record the 30-second version once and listen to it — you will
hear the compound-clause habit flagged in `01-diagnosis.md` §D5 immediately.

### 10 seconds — the handshake

> "Siddharth Bagga. I'm a private-company analyst — valuation and acquisition
> underwriting for lower-middle-market businesses here in Cincinnati."

Stop there. Do not add a third clause. Let them ask.

### 30 seconds — the standard answer to "what do you do?"

> "I'm a private-company analyst. Over the last year I built a small Cincinnati
> company's entire finance function from raw bank statements — then underwrote
> its acquisition pipeline and wrote the Reg D materials for its deal-by-deal
> raises. So I've done the unglamorous version: valuing businesses where there's
> no market price and no clean data.
>
> I finished my MS Finance at UC last year, and I'm finishing a green-card
> process right now, so I'm using the window to go deep on the Cincinnati
> lower-middle market — building out a thesis on [your sector]. What's your
> corner of the market?"

Three moves: evidence → status pre-empted without apology → hand the
conversation back. The question at the end is the whole point.

### 60 seconds — when someone leans in

Add, after "no clean data":

> "The part I found most interesting was the underwriting. We were looking at a
> $6M multi-state pipeline, and the modelling was the easy half — the hard half
> was deciding what normalised EBITDA actually was for a business where the
> owner's comp, the vehicles and three of the vendor relationships were all
> personal. I ended up writing go/no-go memos to our Managing Partner, and what
> I learned is that the memo *is* the deliverable. The model is just what you
> did to be allowed to write it."

**[I]** This paragraph is your strongest sixty seconds. It demonstrates that you
understand where value actually sits in the job, and quality-of-earnings
judgment on owner-operator businesses is the single most-discussed topic among
LMM investors. It also happens to be true and defensible.

### 2 minutes — the career story

Four beats. Do not exceed four.

1. **Origin (20s).** "I did my B.Com in Delhi and got interested in valuation
   through an empirical research project on how people actually make risk
   decisions — primary survey data, not textbook. That's still how I think:
   start with the question, then the evidence."
2. **Training (25s).** "I came to UC for the MS in Finance and added the
   quantitative finance certificate. Capstone was a DCF on S&P Global with three
   others — we came out at $391 against a $510 market price and recommended
   sell. What stuck with me wasn't the answer, it was the sensitivity band: a
   50-basis-point move in WACC moved the whole call. That's when discount rates
   stopped being homework."
3. **The real work (45s).** The 60-second block above.
4. **Now (30s).** "I'm mid green-card process, so I can't take a role yet. Rather
   than wait, I'm using the window deliberately: building out a lower-middle-
   market thesis on [sector] in this region, working through a private-credit
   underwriting case, and meeting the people who actually do this here. When
   I'm authorised I want to be somewhere I'm underwriting deals, not preparing
   to underwrite deals."

**[I]** Beat 4 is doing quiet heavy lifting. "I'm using the window deliberately"
converts an unemployment gap into evidence of exactly the trait — self-directed
work without supervision — that a two-person sponsor shop most needs and can
least verify.

### The interview answer to "Walk me through your background"

Same four beats, but lead with the *seat you want*:

> "Shortest version: I want to be underwriting lower-middle-market deals, and
> I've already done a version of that job. [Beat 3, then beat 2, then beat 4.]"

## §4. What never to say

| Never | Because |
|---|---|
| "Aspiring investment professional" | Describes a wish. You have evidence; use it. |
| "I'm passionate about private equity" | Everyone says it, so it carries no information. |
| "I'm open to any opportunity in finance" | Reads as no thesis about yourself. In a business about having theses, that is disqualifying. |
| "Financial Mathematics" | Not your degree [V]. See `01-diagnosis.md` §B1. |
| "My valuation was within 10% of consensus" | Not supported by your capstone [V]. See §B2. |
| Anything implying you can work now | See §B4. **IMMIGRATION COUNSEL REVIEW REQUIRED.** |
| "I'm just looking to learn / pick your brain" | Signals you will consume time and return nothing. Bring a question only they can answer. |
