# PitchBook Playbook

You have PitchBook via your UC email. This is the single biggest capability
upgrade available to this system — it attacks the two hardest problems in it
(finding real people, and knowing real private-company facts). But it comes with
three constraints that change *how* you should use it, and if you ignore them you
will either waste the access or misuse it.

Read §1 before you log in again.

---

## §1. Three constraints that determine the whole strategy

### Constraint 1 — It is a READING tool, not an export tool

**[R]** University PitchBook accounts are commonly capped at roughly **10 rows of
data per day and 25 rows per month** (company, deal, fund or people data), with
no plugin access and no fund-returns data. Programmatic access, scraping, or
"using computer programming/services to access, gather or copy" the content is
prohibited.
(<https://pitchbook.com/help/university-account-access-guide>,
<https://library.yale.edu/eresources/access/access-pitchbook>)

⚠️ Verify UC's specific limits — they vary by institution — but plan for them
being tight.

**[I] This is the most important practical fact, and it inverts the obvious
plan.** You cannot export the Cincinnati PE universe into your CRM. Any strategy
built on bulk extraction is dead on arrival. What you *can* do is **read deeply
on screen and hand-transcribe the handful of fields that matter** into Sheet 2
and Sheet 4 — which is exactly the 12-minute-per-record protocol this system
already uses. Your CRM needs about ten fields per row, not forty. The download
cap barely binds if you are transcribing judgment rather than harvesting data.

### Constraint 2 — Academic licenses are for academic, non-commercial use

**[R]** University access is licensed for **non-commercial, educational use**.
Some institutions state explicitly that it may not be used "for internships or
commercial ventures," and that users may not use the content in the offering or
solicitation of investments in securities. Accounts are individual and may not be
shared.

**[I] Where this lands for you.** Personal career research and coursework-style
learning are the intended use — it is why business schools buy it. But three
specific things in this system now need care:

| Activity | Status |
|---|---|
| Reading firm and deal data to prepare for a conversation | Normal use |
| Hand-noting facts into your own private research notes | Normal use |
| **Publishing PitchBook data in your public sector thesis** | **Don't.** See §4. |
| **Using it to support any paid or commercial work** | **Don't.** Also an immigration question — see `18-immigration-counsel-questions.md` |
| Sharing your login, or pulling data for someone else | Never |

⚠️ **[REC]** Read UC's own PitchBook terms page before your next session — search
UC Libraries for "PitchBook." If UC's language mirrors the schools that exclude
"internships and commercial ventures," treat job-search use as personal
educational research, keep it private, and do not put PitchBook figures into
anything you publish or hand to a firm.

### Constraint 3 — This access is temporary and may end without warning

**[R]** PitchBook university licenses generally **do not extend to alumni**.
Stanford, Berkeley and Yale all restrict access to currently enrolled students,
faculty and staff, and Stanford states plainly that recent graduates no longer
have access.

You graduated in **May 2025** [V]. That you still have access is either a UC
policy difference or an account that has not yet been swept. Either way:

**[I] Assume it disappears without notice. This is the single most important
scheduling consequence in this file: front-load everything durable.**

The distinction that matters:

| Type of use | Value after access ends |
|---|---|
| **Firm dossiers and a deal map written into your own notes** | **Permanent.** Yours forever. |
| Ad-hoc lookups before a meeting | Zero. Gone with the login. |

So spend weeks 1–4 building the durable assets, and only then settle into
on-demand use. If you do it the other way round and access lapses in month three,
you will have nothing to show for it.

## §2. The five uses that matter, ranked

### USE 1 — Fund-close screening: the hiring signal nobody looks for ★

**[I] This is the highest-value and least obvious use of PitchBook for someone in
your position.**

A fund that has just closed a new vehicle has capital it must deploy, a deal pace
it must raise, and — frequently — a reason to add junior headcount. Almost no
candidate screens for this. Everyone screens job boards, which for two-to-six
person LMM firms are empty by definition (`14-ead-arrival-and-100-day.md`: those
seats are never posted).

**The screen:** Funds → location Ohio / Kentucky / Indiana → fund size under
$500M → close date in the last 18 months → strategy: buyout, growth, mezzanine.

**What you do with it:** any firm on that list moves to the top of your outreach
queue in Sheet 2, with priority score 90+. Your message can then reference
something real and current about their business without being remotely intrusive.

Re-run monthly. It takes fifteen minutes and it is the closest thing to a
proprietary job-lead source you will have.

### USE 2 — Dossiers on your 30 target employers ★

Twenty minutes per firm, ~10 hours total, spread across weeks 1–4. For each,
transcribe into Sheet 4 / Sheet 3 notes:

- Fund family, current fund number, vintage year, fund size
- Sector focus and stated check size
- **Last 5 transactions**: target, date, sector, deal type, size if disclosed
- Any platform-vs-add-on pattern (tells you whether they buy or build)
- Team size and structure
- Whether they have taken on institutional LPs or run deal-by-deal

**[I] Why this is worth ten hours.** It converts every outreach message from
generic to specific. Compare:

> *Generic:* "I'm interested in lower-middle-market private equity in Cincinnati."

> *Specific:* "You've done four add-ons under the same platform since 2024, all
> in the same sector — I've been building a thesis on why that consolidation
> works in this region, and I'd value knowing where my read is wrong."

The second is the `09-networking-system.md` Curiosity script with real fuel in
it. Nothing else in your toolkit produces that.

### USE 3 — The Cincinnati LMM deal map ★

**The screen:** Deals → PE-backed buyout, growth/expansion, and add-on →
location Ohio/Kentucky/Indiana → deal size $5M–$150M → last 3–4 years.

Read the results. Hand-transcribe **40 deals** into Sheet 6 over several weeks:
target, acquirer, date, sector, deal type, size if disclosed, and — the column
that matters — **one sentence on why you think they did it**.

**[I]** This becomes two things at once: the private supplement to your Project 1
sector thesis, and the `08-conversation-portfolio.md` §3 transactions list you
were otherwise going to build by hand from EDGAR. It is also the thing that lets
you say, at an ACG event, *"I've been mapping every sub-$150M deal in the
tri-state for three years — the pattern I can't explain is X."* That sentence
gets you a second conversation with almost anyone in the room.

### USE 4 — People sourcing, verified

PitchBook's people data gives you titles at firms whose websites are thin — and
many LMM firms have deliberately thin websites.

**[REC] But keep the discipline from `05-people-and-firms.md` §3:** PitchBook is
**Tier 3** under `19-verification-protocol.md`, not Tier 1. Its people records go
stale. Use it to *discover*, then confirm the title on the firm's own team page
before you write to someone. Two sources, two minutes, and it is the difference
between a message that lands and one that quietly doesn't.

### USE 5 — Comparable transactions for Projects 2 and 5

Real multiples for your LBO and your valuation work — the thing that ordinarily
separates a student model from a credible one.

⚠️ **But see §4 before putting a single PitchBook multiple into a document you
intend to share.**

### Not worth your time
- Fund returns data — **not available on academic accounts** [R]
- LP/allocator data — interesting, irrelevant to you for years
- VC-stage screening — wrong end of the market for your ranked paths
- General "browsing to learn the industry" — see §5

## §3. Time budget

| Phase | PitchBook time | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Weeks 1–4 (front-load)** | 45 min/day | Use 1 monthly screen, Use 2 dossiers, Use 3 deal map |
| **Weeks 5–12** | 20 min/day | Finish the deal map; pre-meeting prep |
| **Week 13+** | On demand | 20 min before any outreach or meeting; Use 1 monthly |

This fits inside the existing 60–90 minute reputation block. **It does not get
extra time.** PitchBook replaces some of the manual sourcing hours in
`05-people-and-firms.md` §3 — it does not add a fourth daily activity.

## §4. What changes in the rest of the system

### `07-investment-portfolio.md` — the publication rule tightens

**Your Project 1 sector thesis was designed to be published.** That was safe
because every figure came from Census, BLS and EDGAR — public, free,
redistributable, and citable by anyone.

**[REC] Keep it that way.** Build the public version entirely from Tier 1 public
sources. Keep PitchBook-derived deal knowledge in a **private** companion section
you never publish, and use it in conversation as *"from my own research"* rather
than as a cited chart.

**[I] There is an upside to this constraint that is worth seeing.** A thesis
built from County Business Patterns and QCEW is *more* impressive to an LMM
investor than one built from PitchBook, not less — because they already have
PitchBook. What they do not have is someone who went to the primary government
data and counted the companies themselves. Your original plan was already the
better one; PitchBook makes your private understanding deeper without changing
what you publish.

### `19-verification-protocol.md` — where PitchBook sits

**Tier 3 (industry database).** Excellent for discovery and for deal facts;
**not** a primary source. Two specific cautions:

1. PitchBook flags estimated vs. reported figures. **Carry that flag into your
   `Revenue Basis` column** — an estimated revenue is `ESTIMATE`, not `REPORTED`,
   even coming from PitchBook.
2. Deal sizes are often undisclosed and sometimes modelled. If it is not
   disclosed, your sheet says undisclosed.

### `05-people-and-firms.md` — the protocol gets faster, not looser

Step 1 becomes "PitchBook to discover, firm team page to verify." Step 4
(second-sourcing) becomes *more* important, not less, because you are now working
from a database whose people records can be a year stale.

## §5. The trap

**[I] PitchBook is the most seductive productivity trap available to you.**

Screening feels like work. It produces charts, lists, and the sensation of
mastery. Six hours can disappear into it and leave you with nothing that changes
your position — no message sent, no relationship advanced, no memo paragraph
written. And because it *feels* rigorous, it is much harder to notice than an
hour lost on LinkedIn.

Recall the finding in `17-evidence-base.md` §4: in professional domains,
deliberate practice explained **under 1%** of performance variance. Your
diagnosis said your problem is **legibility and access**, not knowledge. More
data does not fix legibility or access. Only sent messages and produced work do.

**The rule: every PitchBook session ends with an artifact.**

```
☐ A row written into Sheet 2, 4 or 6, or
☐ A message sent that the session made specific, or
☐ A paragraph added to a memo
```

No artifact, no session. Put that line at the top of your PitchBook notes doc.

## §6. First session — 90 minutes, do it this week

```
☐ 0-10   Find and read UC's PitchBook terms page. Note the download limits
         and the permitted-use language in Sheet 15.
☐ 10-25  USE 1: run the fund-close screen (OH/KY/IN, <$500M, last 18 months).
         Write every firm into Sheet 3 with priority 90+.
☐ 25-70  USE 2: three dossiers, 15 min each, on your top three target firms
         from 14-ead-arrival-and-100-day.md §5.
☐ 70-85  Using one dossier, write and SEND one Curiosity message that would
         have been impossible an hour ago.
☐ 85-90  Log sources in Sheet 15. Tier 3. Today's date.
```

**[I]** The 70–85 block is the whole point. If you finish the session having
learned a great deal and sent nothing, you have proved §5 rather than avoided it.
