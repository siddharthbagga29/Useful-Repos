# PART V — People: Firm Register, Sourcing Protocol, Relationship Strategy

## §1. Why this file contains a protocol instead of 130 invented names

The master prompt asks for ~130 named individuals with titles, focus areas and
recent activity — and separately, in §39, forbids hallucination. For people at
small private firms those two instructions cannot both be satisfied from model
memory. Titles at a six-person sponsor change without announcement; "recent
activity" is exactly the class of fact most likely to be confabulated.

**The practical argument, not the principled one:** a networking list that is 70%
accurate is worse than no list. Addressing a Principal as a Partner, or citing a
deal that firm did not do, does not produce a correction — it produces silence,
and you never learn why. You would be spending your scarcest asset (first
impressions with a small, interconnected community) on my recall.

So: firms below are real and cited. People you source yourself, from primary
pages, at ~5/hour. Twenty hours of work over six weeks yields 100 verified
contacts, each one already carrying its own source URL.

## §3. The People Sourcing Protocol

**12 minutes per person. Do it in blocks of five.**

**Step 1 — Open the firm's own site (2 min).** Team / Our People / Professionals.
This is a Tier 1 source under `19-verification-protocol.md`: the firm published
it about itself.

> **With PitchBook (`20-pitchbook-playbook.md`):** use PitchBook to *discover*
> people at firms whose websites are thin — many LMM firms keep them deliberately
> sparse — then still open the firm's own team page to confirm the title. Two
> sources, two minutes. Do not skip the confirmation: PitchBook people-records go
> stale, and a wrong title is the most damaging error in cold outreach.

**Step 2 — Capture exactly what is printed (3 min).** Full name as written. Title
*verbatim* — "Principal" is not "Partner", "Vice President" is not "Director",
and getting it wrong is the most common and most damaging cold-outreach error.
Their stated sector focus. Their bio's education line.

**Step 3 — Find the warrant (3 min).** The reason you specifically may write to
this person. Ranked by strength:
1. UC alumnus [V, A2] — strongest available to you, and common in Cincinnati
2. Delhi University / India connection
3. Shared organisation (ACG, CFA Society, Goering)
4. They invest in the sector of your Project 1 thesis
5. They published something you actually read
6. Geography alone — weakest; use only when nothing else exists

**Step 4 — Second-source anything that matters (2 min).** If a fact will appear
in your email, confirm it somewhere other than the team page. For an SEC-
registered adviser, Form ADV on IAPD (<https://adviserinfo.sec.gov>) is
authoritative for AUM, ownership and personnel. For a broker-dealer, FINRA
BrokerCheck. **This is the step that separates you from every other cold
emailer**, and it takes two minutes.

**Step 5 — Write the row (2 min).** Sheet 2, all fields, Verification Status =
VERIFIED, Date Verified = today, Source URL = the team page. Then write the *one
sentence* in "Best reason to contact" — if you cannot write it, do not keep
the row. A person you have no reason to contact is not a contact.

### Where LinkedIn is and is not sufficient

**Acceptable:** discovering that a person exists; checking whether they still
appear to be at the firm; seeing what they post about; finding shared connections.

**Not acceptable, ever, as your only source:** titles, tenure, AUM, fund size,
deal history, education. Profiles are self-authored, frequently stale, and
sometimes aspirational. **[I]** Quoting a stale LinkedIn title back to someone
who was promoted eight months ago tells them precisely how much verification you
do — which is the exact quality they are hiring for.

**Rule:** LinkedIn discovers; primary sources verify.

## §4. Relationship tiers — the point is not 1,000 connections

| Tier | Definition | Target | Cadence | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A — Strategic** | Could materially alter your career: LMM partners, sponsor principals, family-office CIOs, boutique MDs, searchers with capital | 15–20 | Every 30–45 days | Never contact without something to give |
| **B — Connectors** | Could introduce you to an A: senior associates, VPs, lawyers, accountants, lenders, Goering PSR advisors, UC faculty | 30–40 | Every 60 days | Most under-rated tier — see below |
| **C — Peers** | Analysts, associates, MBA candidates, other searchers, CFA candidates | 30–40 | Every 90 days | Your A-tier in ten years |
| **D — Broad** | Useful industry contacts, event acquaintances | Unlimited | Opportunistic | Never actively worked |

**[R] Why B is the highest-yield tier.** Rajkumar et al., *Science* 2022 — the
largest causal test of the strength-of-weak-ties hypothesis, randomised across
20M+ LinkedIn users, 2B new ties and 600k job transitions — found an **inverted-U**:
weaker ties transmit jobs better than strong ties, *up to a point*, after which
returns diminish. The most useful contacts were moderately weak — roughly ten
shared connections, infrequent interaction. Not your closest friends. Not
strangers. (<https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abl4476>)

**[I] Practical implication, which is not the usual one.** The lesson is *not*
"collect strangers." It is that the highest-value relationship you can
deliberately build is the **acquaintance you maintain** — someone you meet at ACG,
speak to three times a year, and who thinks of you when a deal team needs an
analyst. That is exactly what a 30/60/90-day cadence in Sheet 2 manufactures.
Confidence: high (large randomised experiment, though on one platform, in a
job-transmission setting rather than private-markets hiring specifically).

**[R]** Wolff & Moser, *JAP* 2009 — longitudinal, three waves — found networking
predicted both concurrent salary and **salary growth rate** over time, with
*building* and *maintaining* contacts separable from *using* them.
**[I] Implication:** the maintenance behaviour, not the outreach burst, is what
compounds. This is the argument for the CRM. Confidence: moderate-high (German
sample, self-reported networking).

**[R]** Burks, Cowgill, Hoffman & Housman, *QJE* 2015 — referred workers are
10–30% less likely to quit, perform better on high-impact metrics, and are more
profitable per worker, with the effect driven by referrals from high-quality
employees.
**[I] Implication:** a referral is not a courtesy — it is the referrer spending
their own credibility, and it materially changes your odds. It is also why every
A-tier relationship must be built to the point where the person would *want* to
spend that credibility. Confidence: high (nine firms, large administrative
datasets; external validity to LMM PE is an inference, not a finding).
