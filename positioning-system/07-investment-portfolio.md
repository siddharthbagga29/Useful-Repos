# PART VII — Investment Portfolio

## §1. Why six projects, not ten — and why not the list in the prompt

The prompt proposes ten projects and invites me to disagree. I disagree on three
points.

**1. A public-company DCF is not a differentiator for you.** Every finance
graduate has one; you already have one [V, A8]; and it demonstrates the skill
your transcript is weakest in [V, A5] against a benchmark (market price) that
makes you look wrong by construction. It belongs in the portfolio as *table
stakes*, not as the lead.

**2. Ten projects at portfolio depth is roughly 350 hours.** Across six months
alongside networking, that is the whole budget, and half-finished projects are
worse than none — you cannot defend them under questioning, which is the only
purpose they serve.

**3. The prompt's list is optimised for a banking interview.** Your ranked paths
are independent sponsors, LMM PE, search funds and valuation advisory
(`01-diagnosis.md` §E). Those audiences want to know whether you can *find and
judge* a private business, not whether you can lever a public one.

**Selection rule:** each project must be defensible for 30 minutes of hostile
questioning, must demonstrate a skill your transcript does not already prove, and
must be *discussable* — usable in a conversation without a laptop.

## §2. The six projects, in build order

### PROJECT 1 — Cincinnati LMM Sector Thesis ★ the differentiator
| | |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | One 4-digit NAICS sector, four-county Cincinnati region |
| **Hours** | 35–45 |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 — the analysis is easy; the sourcing discipline is not |
| **Skills shown** | Original primary-source research, market sizing, sector judgment, regional knowledge |
| **Output** | 8–12 page memo + one-page summary + the underlying data workbook |
| **Shareable?** | **Yes — publish it.** No named private company's financials, no confidentiality exposure |
| **Verify by** | Every number traces to a BLS/Census/EDGAR URL in an appendix |

**Sector selection — pick one:**

| Sector | Case for | Case against |
|---|---|---|
| **Logistics & 3PL** (NAICS 4885, 4931) | Cincinnati is a genuine national logistics node; TQL is the region's largest private company [R]; Taylor Logistics is 7th-generation family-owned [R]; highly fragmented, classic roll-up shape | Crowded with sponsors already |
| **Specialty manufacturing** (NAICS 332, 333) | Deep regional base; ageing owners; the archetypal LMM buyout | Cyclical; capital-intensive; hard to judge from outside |
| **Healthcare services** (NAICS 621) | RC Capital and Brixey & Meyer both target it [R]; you would be speaking directly to two local funds' thesis | Reimbursement complexity is a real barrier for a newcomer |
| **Commercial & industrial services** (NAICS 561) | **Closest to your actual experience [V, A10]** — recurring revenue, owner-operated, fragmented, low capital intensity, and you have already underwritten a multi-state pipeline in adjacent territory | Least "impressive"-sounding, which is irrelevant |

**[REC] Choose commercial & industrial services.** It is the sector where your
Turnkey experience makes your judgment *earned* rather than researched, and where
you can say "here is what I learned underwriting these" instead of "here is what
I read." Authenticity is the whole asset. Pick a different sector only if you
have a genuine reason.

**Memo structure:** Question → Why this sector, this region, now → How many
companies exist (Census CBP by employee band, cited) → Local demand trend (BLS
QCEW, cited) → Fragmentation and consolidation evidence → What a buyer actually
underwrites (the owner-comp / customer-concentration / subcontractor issues you
have seen first-hand) → Where the thesis breaks → Fourteen named companies with
ownership type and source → Appendix of every URL.

**Source pack to download in hour 4:** Census CBP for your NAICS × Hamilton,
Butler, Warren, Clermont counties; BLS QCEW same cut; one public comparable's
latest 10-K from EDGAR; the relevant trade association's most recent public
industry report.

### PROJECT 2 — LBO of a Real LMM-Scale Target
| | |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | A public company small enough to be a plausible LMM/lower-mid target, or a hypothetical built from your Project 1 sector economics |
| **Hours** | 30–35 |
| **Difficulty** | 4/5 |
| **Skills shown** | Full debt schedule with revolver and cash sweep, IRR/MOIC bridge, **returns attribution** |
| **Output** | Model + 3-page investment committee memo |
| **Shareable?** | Model on request; memo yes |
| **Verify by** | Balance sheet ties; sources = uses; returns attribution sums to total MOIC |

**The one thing that matters:** the **returns attribution bridge** — how much of
the MOIC came from EBITDA growth, margin expansion, multiple expansion, and debt
paydown. Most candidate LBOs produce an IRR and stop. Attribution is what an
investor actually asks about, because it reveals whether the return is earned or
assumed. If multiple expansion is doing the work, say so and explain why you
distrust your own model.

### PROJECT 3 — Private Credit Underwriting Memo ★ the scarce one
| | |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | A unitranche or mezzanine facility for a company in your Project 1 sector |
| **Hours** | 25–30 |
| **Difficulty** | 4/5 |
| **Skills shown** | Credit structuring, covenant design, downside modelling, recovery analysis |
| **Output** | 5–6 page credit memo with term sheet |
| **Shareable?** | Yes |
| **Verify by** | Fixed-charge coverage and leverage calculations reconcile; downside case actually breaches your own covenant |

**[I] Why this is the highest-leverage project on the list.** Almost no
candidate has one. Path #6 (private credit) is your least-competed target, and
Northcreek is a real Cincinnati SBIC [R] with a small team. Equally important: an
equity investor reads a credit memo and sees someone who thinks about *downside
first* — the rarest and most valued disposition in a 23-year-old.

Build the downside case first, then the base case. Set the covenant so the
downside breaches it. Then write the paragraph on what you would do at that
point. That paragraph is the entire memo.

## §4. Verification — the part that makes these credible

1. **Arithmetic:** every model gets a check tab — balance sheet ties, sources =
   uses, cash flow reconciles, returns attribution sums.
2. **Assumption register:** one tab listing every assumption, its source, and the
   output's sensitivity to it. **[I]** Your own capstone is the argument for this:
   a 50bp WACC change moved that call from $391 to $565 [V, A8]. Lead with your
   sensitivities and you look rigorous; hide them and you look naive.
3. **External review:** before any project is called finished, one person who
   does this professionally reads it. Ask a UC Lindner faculty member, a
   B-tier CRM contact, or a peer. **This is also a networking action** — asking
   someone to critique your work is the single most reliable way to get a
   substantive second conversation with a busy professional.
4. **The hostile-question test:** write the five questions you least want to be
   asked. Answer them in writing. If you cannot, the project is not done.

## §5. Confidentiality, IP, and what you must not publish

⚠️ Read before publishing anything.

**Absolute prohibitions:**
- **Nothing from Turnkey Services Pro.** Not the models, not the valuations, not
  the pipeline, not the Reg D materials, not the client names, not anonymised
  versions detailed enough to identify. That work belongs to your employer, was
  produced under an employment relationship, and likely under confidentiality
  obligations. You may *describe your role* in general terms; you may not
  reproduce work product. **[REC]** If you are unsure where the line is, ask the
  Managing Partner in writing what you may reference. Get the answer in writing.
- **Nothing from Cerity Partners.** Client data, reporting formats, processes.

**Publish freely:** analysis of public companies from public filings; sector
research from government data; composite/hypothetical cases clearly labelled as
such; your own criteria and frameworks.

**Do not publish, share 1:1 only:** any valuation of a *named private company*.
**[I]** Publishing "XYZ Manufacturing is worth $14M" carries three distinct risks —
the owner may see it and be justifiably angry; it can be read as investment advice
or as soliciting; and it makes every LMM investor wonder what you would publish
about *their* portfolio. Name companies in a *sourcing list*; never attach a value
to a named private business in public.

**Label everything.** Every published piece carries: *"Independent research based
solely on publicly available information. Not investment advice. Not produced for
or on behalf of any company."*

**Two immigration-adjacent cautions — IMMIGRATION COUNSEL REVIEW REQUIRED:**
- Do not accept payment, equity, or anything of value for any of this work.
- Do not produce analysis *at the request of* a company, even unpaid, even framed
  as a "sample" or "trial project." Self-directed research published on your own
  initiative is a materially different thing from work performed for a firm — and
  only your attorney can tell you where that line sits. See
  `18-immigration-counsel-questions.md` Q5–Q8.
